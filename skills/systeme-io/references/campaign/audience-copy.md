# Understand the reader and write the offer

## Research before drafting

Identify the job the visitor is trying to finish, what they have already tried, the cost of delay, the objection to this offer and the next sensible action. Separate a beginner learning request from a buying comparison. Search queries, customer questions and interviews can inform the brief; do not claim search volume or customer consensus without evidence.

Read current official product sources for features and limits. Use the user's actual experience for first-person statements. When evidence is absent, write an opinion as an opinion or a proposal as a proposal, not a result.

## Build the message hierarchy

| Section | Reader question | Copy job |
|---|---|---|
| Opening | Is this relevant to me? | Name the audience/problem and a credible desired outcome |
| Explanation | What is it and how does it help? | Connect real features to practical tasks |
| Evidence | Why should I believe it? | Use authentic examples, demonstration or attributable proof |
| Fit and limits | Is it right for my situation? | Explain requirements, trade-offs and who should choose another route |
| Offer | What do I receive and what does it cost? | State actual deliverables and terms clearly |
| Next action | What happens when I click? | Match CTA wording and destination |
| Questions | What uncertainty remains? | Answer specific objections without filling space |

Choose only the sections needed; a link-in-bio page usually needs fewer than a course sales page. Do not pad a page to meet a word count.

Rewrite generic claims into concrete tasks: "Grow your business" can become "Collect enquiries from your bio page and send each person the promised guide." Only describe a benefit the offer actually supports.

Use action-oriented CTAs such as "Get the checklist" or "Start the free course" when truthful. Sentence case is a sensible default; consistency and legibility matter more than capitalizing every word. Place the CTA where a reader has enough information to decide. Supporting copy should clarify the next step, not pressure the reader.

Map H1/H2/H3 to real questions and include topic terms naturally. Compare copy versions against the same brief: relevance, specificity, credibility, clarity, friction and continuity into follow-up. Do not imply a copy score predicts sales.
